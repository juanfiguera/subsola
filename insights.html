    Lead pipes into buildings

    New York, DC, San Diego, Seattle, Denver and Dallas publish an inventory of the service lines that run from the water main into each building. In New York, tax lots, of the total, still have a lead line.

    Each city counts differently

    New York counts tax lots. DC counts premises and shows only lead it has verified; suspected lead is under not yet known, and DC Water files galvanized pipe under lead rather than apart. San Diego counts water meters. Seattle publishes only the material of its own part of each service, up to the owner's pipe, and no lead classification. Denver Water counts premises, files galvanized pipe downstream of lead under lead, and puts the lines it calls likely lead under not yet known. Dallas Water Utilities counts services, inside the city's streets, on the same template as San Diego.

    Galvanized pipe can carry lead it picked up from older pipe upstream.

    Permits, not shovels

    A permit is permission to open the street, not proof that it was opened. Permits that never cut the street, such as New York's permits to open a manhole or store material, are left out.

    One dig

    One company's permits for one utility on one block, with permits less than 30 days apart counted together. The one-year share leaves out digs from the last year of records, and the three-year share the last three, since what came after them can't be seen yet.

    Cities left out of the dig figures

    Los Angeles and San Diego file one permit per job and often place it on a whole street rather than a block, so their digs aren't comparable and aren't charted.
